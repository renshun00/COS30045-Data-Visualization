Why It Matters

Energy costs add up fast

With electricity prices in Australia rising year on year, knowing your appliance usage is the first step to smarter bills.

🏠

Household Consumption

The average Australian home uses around 18–20 kWh per day. Appliances account for roughly 60% of that — with heating, cooling, and refrigeration leading the way.

📺

Televisions & Entertainment

Modern televisions have become significantly more efficient, but larger screens and longer viewing hours can still add up to $80–$150 per year on your power bill.

❄️

Air Conditioning

Reverse-cycle air conditioners are the single biggest appliance energy user, consuming 1–3 kWh per hour of operation. Choosing a high-star-rated model can save hundreds annually.

🌞

Solar & Renewables

Over 3 million Australian homes now have rooftop solar. Understanding when your appliances run helps you maximise self-consumption and minimise grid import costs.

🔌

Standby Power

Devices on standby collectively waste 8–15% of a household's electricity. Switching off at the wall — particularly for TVs and set-top boxes — can cut dozens of dollars from your bill.

⭐

Energy Star Ratings

Australia's mandatory star rating label makes it easy to compare appliances. Each additional star typically represents a 25–30% reduction in running costs over the appliance's lifetime.

Frequently Asked Questions

Everything you need to know about household appliance energy consumption in Australia.

  • Electricity in Australia is priced per kilowatt-hour (kWh) and varies by state, retailer, and plan type. As of 2024, the national average sits around 28–36 cents per kWh for residential customers, with South Australia typically the highest and Queensland among the lowest. Most plans include a fixed daily supply charge plus a usage rate. Time-of-use tariffs charge more during peak hours (typically 3–9 pm on weekdays) and less during off-peak periods — relevant if you run heavy appliances like dishwashers or washing machines.
  • The Energy Rating Label is a mandatory requirement for most appliances sold in Australia, administered under the Equipment Energy Efficiency (E3) programme. The label shows a star rating (1–6 stars, sometimes up to 10 for the most efficient models) and an annual energy consumption figure in kWh. More stars = lower running costs. A 6-star fridge uses roughly half the electricity of a 2-star fridge of the same size. When buying a new appliance, the upfront premium for a higher star-rated model is often recouped within 2–4 years through lower bills.
  • In a typical Australian home, the biggest energy consumers (in order) are:
    1. Heating and cooling (air conditioners, reverse-cycle systems) — up to 40%
    2. Water heating — 20–25%
    3. Refrigerators & freezers — 8–10% (run 24/7)
    4. Cooking appliances — 5–7%
    5. Lighting — 5–7%
    6. Televisions & entertainment — 4–6%
    7. Washing machines & dryers — 4–5%
  • Standby power (sometimes called "vampire power") accounts for 8–15% of household electricity use. Practical steps to reduce it include: switching appliances off at the wall when not in use (especially TVs, gaming consoles, and set-top boxes), using smart power strips that cut power to idle devices, enabling power-saving modes on computers and monitors, and replacing older appliances with newer models that have lower standby draw. The Australian Government's Your Energy Savings website provides a detailed standby power usage guide with typical wattages for common devices.
  • Solar panels can dramatically reduce — but rarely eliminate — electricity bills for most Australian households. The savings depend on your system size, roof orientation, local solar irradiance, and your household's daytime usage patterns. The key principle is self-consumption: energy your solar panels generate and your home uses directly is effectively free; energy exported to the grid earns a feed-in tariff (currently 5–12 c/kWh in most states), which is much lower than the import rate you pay. Shifting high-draw appliances (dishwashers, washing machines, EV charging) to daylight hours maximises your savings.
  • It depends on your electricity tariff. If you are on a flat-rate plan, the time of day makes no difference to your bill. If you are on a time-of-use (TOU) tariff, off-peak rates (typically overnight and on weekends) can be 30–50% cheaper than peak rates. Appliances like dishwashers and washing machines with delay-start features can be programmed to run during the cheapest window. However, if you have solar and no battery storage, running appliances during daylight hours is typically even cheaper than an overnight off-peak rate.
